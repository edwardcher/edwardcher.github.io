Beyond the Terminal: Learning to Appreciate Mechanical Time

A personal reflection on watchmaking, thoughtful design, and what a long career in technology has taught me to appreciate.

I have spent more than 25 years in IT. I started out writing software, moved through integration and security work, and eventually into architecture. Much of that time has been spent figuring out why things fail, how to make them work together, and how to build something that will still make sense years later.

Somewhere along the way, I became interested in mechanical watches.

Not as a replacement for technology. My phone tells time far more accurately, and it does a hundred other things besides. I simply started paying attention to how a watch was made, and found there was more to appreciate than I expected.

From function to design

Earlier in my career, I was mostly interested in whether something worked. Could the code run? Would the integration hold? Could a system recover when something went wrong?

Those questions still matter to me. But experience has changed the way I look at things. I now spend more time thinking about the decisions behind a design: why one approach is simpler to maintain, why a small detail matters, and whether the result is likely to last.

That is part of what draws me to watchmaking.

A mechanical movement is a collection of small parts that have to work together. The mainspring stores energy. The gear train transfers it. The escapement releases it in measured steps. None of this is necessary to know the time today, which is precisely why I find the care behind it interesting.

It is engineering with its workings close enough to see and, in some cases, feel.

The details grow on you

My interest did not arrive all at once. At first I noticed the dial. Later I began to appreciate the proportions of the case, the way the hands were shaped, the feel of the crown, and the finishing on the movement.

The NOMOS Zürich Worldtimer in these photographs captures some of that appeal for me. Having worked with people across different time zones for years, its world-time function is something I can relate to. But what interests me most is how the watch makes a complicated mechanism feel so straightforward.

The Zürich is a good example of thoughtful mechanical design. A pusher advances the local-hour display and changes the city shown for the selected time zone, while a separate 24-hour display keeps track of home time. Underneath, the automatic movement has to coordinate those indications with the ordinary business of keeping time. It is a clever piece of engineering, but you don’t need to understand the mechanism to use it. That, to me, is the point of good design: the complexity is there when you want to appreciate it, not in your way when you don’t.

I am not interested in turning every small interest into a technical analysis. Sometimes I just enjoy wearing a well-designed object. But I do find myself noticing the same qualities I look for in a good system: clarity, purpose, and attention to the parts most people never see.

A different way of looking at time

IT is an industry that rarely stands still. There is always another release, another vulnerability, another platform, another urgent problem to solve. I still enjoy that pace, and I still like getting my hands dirty with code and tools.

After enough years, though, I have also learned to value things that do not need constant reinvention.

A mechanical watch needs care. It is not perfect, and it will never win a contest with a digital clock for accuracy. Yet a good movement can be maintained, repaired, and kept running for a very long time. I like that idea.

It reminds me that progress is not only about making things faster or adding more features. Sometimes it is about making thoughtful choices and doing the work properly.

This is a small detour from what I usually write about here. Most of this site will remain about trusted identity, security, systems, and the tools I use to build them. But mechanical watches have become one of the ways I appreciate engineering when I am away from the terminal.

And after a long working day, that is enough.
